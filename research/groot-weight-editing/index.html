Editing GR00T's weights tripled its colour sorting ability, with no fine-tuning

Abdul Rahim Mirani · November 2026

Follow-up to Directly editing a VLA's weights improved its performance, with no fine-tuning, the same experiment on π0.5.

In my last write-up, Opus 5.5 fixed π0.5's colour sorting by editing four attention heads. This time I gave it the same experiment on a different robot policy, NVIDIA's GR00T N1.7, and it ran the whole thing on its own. GR00T fails for a different reason: it puts blue blocks in the wrong bin. Opus 5.5 traced that to two attention heads and turned them up 3×. No training and no fine-tuning: sorting went from 4 to 12 successes out of 30. Unlike with π0.5, the edit has a cost: grasps got slightly less precise, and a second task got worse.

The edit was designed using only the colour-sorting task. The mugs task played no part in choosing it and was only checked for harm. All before and after results come from 30 object placements per task that were not used while designing the edit.

03

How Opus 5.5 found the fix

The problem: “right bin” barely registers

π0.5 failed at sorting because it carried blocks too low and caught the bin rims. GR00T doesn't: it carries high and places cleanly. Its failure is the destination. In the original model's tests, 21 blocks went into the wrong bin, and every one of them was a blue block going left.

Opus 5.5 replayed the moment each carry starts through the model with different instructions, and read off which bin the arm heads for:

Instruction givenBlue goes left
The task's: blue → right bin, yellow → left bin53%
Swapped: blue → left bin, yellow → right bin99%
Only “Put the blue blocks in the right bin.”58%

48 recorded carry starts, each with 4 different random seeds.

So GR00T understands colour: told to put blue on the left, it does so 99% of the time. What's weak is the word “right”. Even an instruction that only says “right bin” sends blue left more than half the time. The fix to aim for: make “right bin” count, without changing anything else.

The fix: two “right bin” dials

  1. 1Test each layerOpus 5.5 turned each layer's attention off, then doubled it, and measured one thing: which bin the arm heads for. Two late layers of the language model, 12 and 13, moved it the most consistently, in both directions.
  2. 2Find the dialsInside those layers, two heads act like a “right bin” dial: turn them down and blue goes left more, turn them up and it goes right.
  3. 3Turn them upMultiplying both by 3 sends blue to the correct bin in 95% of the recorded moments. In the full tests, not one block went to the wrong bin.

Measured while designing the edit, on recorded inputs from the original model. “Other movement” is how far the arm's planned movement shifts at moments before a grasp, where the bin shouldn't matter. The test results are in the Results section above.

This changes 524,288 of the model's 3.46 billion weights. The edited model is saved as an ordinary checkpoint and runs with the standard code, with no extra hooks at inference.

The side effect

The two heads don't only carry “right bin”. Turned up 3×, they shift the gripper about half a centimetre toward the robot's right and slightly higher in general. Carrying blocks to a bin doesn't care about that. Grasping a mug by its rim or picking up a banana does: the edited model closes its gripper a few millimetres off and hooks the mug or misses the banana more often.

Where the edit lives in GR00T

GR00T is a vision-language-action model with two parts. A vision-language backbone (the first 16 layers of NVIDIA's Cosmos-Reason2 2B) reads the camera images and the instruction. An action head, a diffusion transformer with 32 layers, turns the backbone's output and the robot's state into movement. Both edited heads are in the backbone, which has 16 heads per layer.

05

Limits

  • Simulation only, one scene per task. A real robot is untested.
  • The edit removes the wrong-bin failure, but sorting still fails 18 of 30 episodes, mostly because the arm stops grasping after one or two blocks.
  • Unlike the π0.5 edit, this one does not carry over to other tasks, and it makes precise grasps slightly worse.
  • The second task is mugs in a bin instead of the π0.5 study's cups in a basket, so that comparison is not like for like.
  • Opus 5.5 found the heads by testing many candidates against one measurement, not by guessing in one shot.
